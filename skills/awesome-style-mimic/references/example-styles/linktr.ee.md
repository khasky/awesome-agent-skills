# Style: linktr.ee

Generated from 133 pages (100% of non-serial site, plus sampled quotas from 13 templated series) on 2026-08-09.

## Voice profile

Linktree writes as a competent, busy peer who has already built an audience and wants friction removed, not concepts explained. The author is second-person to the bone: "you" and "your" carry nearly every sentence, and the product is written as something the reader already owns — *your Linktree*, a countable object you put things *on* and share *from*. The company appears as "we" only in three narrow jobs — owning a tool ("our analytics tools"), recommending ("we recommend organizing your products into collections"), and absorbing work ("we'll take care of the rest") — plus in mission, culture, and legal copy. It never appears in a performance claim; numbers do that.

The register is casual-professional with contractions always on, and it does not stiffen for money, restrictions, or contracts: the Terms open with a cheery two-sentence welcome, exclamation mark included, before any obligation appears. Warmth substitutes for jokes. Where another brand would be funny, Linktree is reassuring — a short confident aside after the explanation ("That's a win-win.", "No more unnecessary cold DMs."), never a pun in a product blurb.

The one place the voice fully unbuttons is the Creator Reports, which are wry, mildly profane ("F the algorithm", "get your sh\*t together"), and openly hostile to algorithms and "the social media overlords" — never to creators, never to the reader. Antagonists are always third-person systems; the reader is always on the winning side.

## Tone rules

**Do**

- Address one named persona from the first sentence ("As a realtor or real estate marketer, you're presented with the challenge of…"); never write to a general audience.
- Say what becomes possible, not what the reader should do better. Copy grants capability; it does not instruct or scold.
- State the benefit as a transformation the reader already half-owns: `turn X into Y`, where X is something they have and Y is money, leads, or reach.
- De-risk every promise in the same breath: "Start a free trial. Cancel anytime.", "try Pro free for 7 days… cancel anytime or switch to Free."
- Attribute limits to their real owner and offer a consolation path: "This is due to restrictions set by our third-party payment processor" → "you can still share free digital downloads."
- Put hard numbers where an adjective wants to go: "up to 3x more clicks", "70M+ Linkers", "1.8 clicks per visit".
- Footnote every statistic and every limit with an asterisk line, usually closing "See here for more details."
- Concede before rebutting in objection sections ("Though template-based websites admittedly don't have the same functionality as…").
- Keep contractions inside legal, fee, and policy copy.

**Don't**

- Don't use corporate abstraction. Absent across the entire corpus: *leverage, utilize, robust, solution(s), seamless integration, cutting-edge, best-in-class, world-class, revolutionary, game-changing, state-of-the-art, synergy, holistic, ecosystem, empower* (outside mission lines), *delve, elevate, unlock your potential, in today's fast-paced world, at the end of the day*. Note *solutions* survives only as a page-path segment, never as a word the body copy uses.
- Don't hedge. No *might, could potentially, we believe you'll, arguably*. Hedges are quarantined to fee, payout, and eligibility text where they are legally load-bearing.
- Don't sell with fear or scarcity. No *limited time, don't miss out, before it's too late*, no "you're falling behind" framing.
- Don't name or disparage a competitor. It is always "traditional website builders", "other platforms", "random QR generators".
- Don't announce ("We're excited to announce…"). Product news uses third-person Linktree: "Today, Linktree is introducing…".
- Don't use *please* in a procedure step. Steps are bare imperatives.
- Don't say *user* for the reader's people. They are *your audience*, then *visitors*, *customers*, *followers*, *fans*.
- Don't write an "In conclusion" summary. Pages end on a CTA or a routing list.

## Lexicon

**The load-bearing phrases**

- `your Linktree` — the product as the reader's possession and a place: *on* your Linktree, *right from* your Linktree, *add it to* your Linktree.
- `link in bio` (lowercase common noun; `link-in-bio` and `bio link` both occur — the hyphenation is genuinely inconsistent).
- `Linkers` — the branded word for users, always capitalized, usually modified ("comedy Linkers", "foodie Linkers", "70M+ Linkers").
- `one link` / `one place` — the consolidation claim, endlessly re-cut: "one simple link", "all in one place", "one powerful place", "one platform, not five separate tools".
- `turn X into Y` — the single most repeated construction on the site: "Turn visitors into leads", "turn followers into paying clients", "Turn comments into customers", "Turn QR scans into sales".
- Friction-negation as benefit: "No code, no developers", "no data expertise required", "No store to build.", "Never update the link in your bio again!", "without complicated setups or high platform fees".

**Ease and speed stack** (appears in nearly every block): in seconds, in minutes, in under a minute, in just a few clicks, instantly, easily, simply, effortlessly, on autopilot, hands-free, without lifting a finger, while you sleep, 3 simple steps.

**Credibility intensifiers** doing the anti-hype work: `actually` ("what's actually working", "what creators actually earn"), `genuinely` ("genuinely useful"), `real` ("real inquiries", "real income", "real signal, not just vanity metrics").

**Product nouns** are Title Case proper nouns used bare, without "the": Linktree Shops, Digital Products, Social Planner, Link Apps, Audience Manager, Auto-Replies, Bookings, Insights Chat, Sponsored Links, Music Links, Commerce Links, Linktree Pro/Starter/Premium/Free.

**Third-party names as trust proxies**, never explained and never normalized (`beehiiv` stays lowercase): Mailchimp, Kit, Klaviyo, Canva, Google Sheets, Stripe, Spotify, Instagram, TikTok.

**Warm concrete metaphors** instead of abstraction: "your corner of the internet", "your digital home base", "your launchpad", "a control center", "digital universe", "take your Linktree IRL".

**Reproduce, don't fix — real house inconsistencies**

- Spelling is mixed AU/US, often inside one page: `organise`/`organize`, `centralised`/`centralized`, `monetise`/`monetize`, plus surviving `whilst`, `enquiry`, `humour`, `Centre`, `licence`. Marketing tilts US; legal and transparency copy tilts AU.
- The serial comma is inconsistent — usually present in verb triads ("curate, grow, and monetize"), usually absent in brand lists ("Instagram, TikTok, YouTube and more").
- Dash typography is unstable: spaced en dash ` – `, spaced em dash ` — `, and bare hyphen all do the same job. Pick one per document; the site does not.
- Curly and straight apostrophes mix within single pages.

## Rhythm & syntax

**Sentences** run 12–25 words in body copy, median ~16. Long sentences are list-bearing, not clause-nested — subordination is one level deep, led by *Whether / When / If / Once / With*. Legal copy is the exception at 35–55 words, still built from short clauses joined by *and* / *or*.

**Paragraphs** are 1–3 sentences. Nothing runs four without a heading, a break, or a list. The **one-sentence paragraph is the house beat**, used as a turn or a landing: "That's what Linktree sees." / "Representation matters." / "Instagram." Data and news posts run 40–60% single-sentence paragraphs.

**Dominant cadence: long setup → short punch.** A context sentence establishes the situation; a fragment resolves it.

**The two-beat headline** is the signature: two short clauses, the second reframing the first — "Pick your plan. Make it yours." / "Sell goods. Take payments." / "More ways to share. Smarter ways to grow." / "Questions? Answered".

**Headline fragments carry a terminal period** — "Your growth starts here." / "Never miss a posting day again." / "Own your audience." Noun-phrase headings drop it. Question headings keep the question mark.

**Rule of three** is the default grouping: "create, curate and sell", "plan, optimize, and auto-post", "Post, Hashtag, Grow", "no cost, no hassle and no risk". Triads often add "and more" as a fourth escape hatch.

**Rhetorical question as pivot**, usually a fragment answered immediately: "The good news?" / "The result?" / "No Canva account? No problem." / "Still tossing up between…?"

**Sentence-initial conjunctions are standard.** But, And, Plus, So, Instead of, Ultimately, Remember — this is the main pacing tool.

**The dash hangs the payoff clause** off the end of a sentence: "– no data expertise required", "— so you keep more of what you earn", "– all from one place". Nearly every dash introduces a reassurance or a concrete instance.

**Punctuation specifics.** Colons introduce almost every list and split most headlines. Ellipsis sets up a payoff or a joke in the blog voice ("you can do just this… no viral trends or dancing required"). Single quotes scare-quote a term the reader may not own ('microsites', 'dream' clients). Parentheses carry glosses, formats, and the occasional aside — `(e.g., …)` always with the comma, `i.e.` preferred over `that is`. **Exclamation marks are rationed**: near-zero in feature and marketing bodies (two in fourteen feature pages), reserved for help-article completion lines, affirmative FAQ openers ("Yes!"), and jokes in the reports.

**Numbers are always numerals**, compressed and unit-fused: 70M+, 1.7 billion, 12.5%, 3x, ~$4,800/year, $10.5k, 80–95%, 480 every second. Approximation `~` and multiplication `×` are both in the house set.

## Structure

**Marketing / feature page** — one template, reused with near-mechanical fidelity:

1. Eyebrow kicker naming the category (casing varies wildly: `TRACK YOUR PERFORMANCE`, `how it works`, `Shoppable socials`).
2. H1 benefit headline — verb-first imperative or a "Turn X into Y", often with a terminal period.
3. Subhead: 1–2 sentences naming the benefit and concrete features, never the mechanism.
4. CTA button, then an asterisked legal microcopy line.
5. Social-proof strip: "Trusted by 70M+" plus a bare roster of slash-prefixed handles of household-name creators and brands, with no other text.
6. 3–6 alternating blocks, each in a fixed four-part shape: **kicker label → headline → 1–2 sentence body → CTA**. Every block ends in a CTA; none merely informs.
7. Numbered "How it works", almost always three steps, one sentence each.
8. Testimonials: quote → name → role.
9. Closing mini-hero, then "Frequently asked questions" (or its playful variant "Questions? Answered").

**One CTA string per page, repeated verbatim 5–10 times** — "Get started for free", "Try 7 Days Free", "Claim your Linktree". The hero may carry a task-specific variant, then the page settles on one string and never varies it.

**Blog article** — category tag → H1 → one-sentence deck restating the promise → `date · N min read` → Contents → body → CTA → keyword internal-link cluster → `YOU MAY ALSO LIKE`. Titles split into two families: SEO posts are keyword-front Title Case with a benefit tail ("25 Real Estate Instagram Post Ideas That Attract Buyers & Sellers"); editorial and data posts are colon-split and often sentence case ("Not All Clicks Are Equal: A Year of Linktree Traffic, by Source").

Intro is three moves in 2–4 short paragraphs: reader's pain or a surprising number → pivot ("The good news?", "That is, until you find Linktree.") → what the piece delivers. H2s are the search query written out, starting Why / What / How. **Product placement is late and honest** — in "where to sell" sections Linktree is the last item in a neutral list, each option given real downsides. Exactly one CTA per article, terminal, soft, containing "free".

**Help article** — task-shaped H1 ("How to add a Contact Form to your Linktree") → one-sentence outcome deck → date → Table of contents → orientation paragraph ending on a payoff clause ("so you can focus on creating") → optional "How does it work?" → numbered bare imperatives with nested bullets → confirmation sentence ("Your Digital Product link is now live on your Linktree!") → Related Articles.

**Policy and legal** — H1 as a plain noun phrase, dateline immediately under it, one scope paragraph, then question-form headings ("What are cookies?", "How can law enforcement agencies request information from us?"). Community Standards uses bare topic nouns and the imperative "Don't use Linktree to…", with permissions as a two-column contract, `You may:` / `You may not:`. Sensitive prohibitions get a softening rationale first; severe ones stay flat ("Linktree has a no tolerance policy for child harm.").

**Outros never summarize.** Marketing ends on a CTA band; help ends on Related Articles; trust-center pages end on an `Other options:` triage list; the long-form reports explicitly hand the reader's time back, pivot on a "Here's the bottom line:" line, and close on three imperative clauses.

## Formatting habits

- **No bold or italics in body prose.** Emphasis is structural — heading hierarchy, tile titles, caps kickers, short fragments. The one sanctioned bold is in help articles, marking UI strings the reader must click: **+ Add**, **Continue**, **Save**.
- **Marketing copy has no bullet lists.** Parallel content is rendered as tiles: a short title line plus one sentence. Inline enumeration is a comma run ending in "and more".
- **Blog copy runs on the bold-label bullet**: `**Mobile-first design:** Linktree is inherently mobile-first, which means…` Lists are 3–6 items (5 is the mode) and always introduced by a colon-terminated stem ("Realtors use Linktree to:").
- **Help copy uses numbered lists** for sequential UI actions, nested bullets for choices inside a step.
- **Emoji: effectively banned in brand prose.** Zero in features, marketing, solutions, marketplace, blog (one 🏳️‍🌈 in a Pride post), and policy (one ✅ on a trial line). The only systematic use is a closed callout set in help articles: `📝 Note:` (limits), `💡 Tip:` (optimization or upsell), `➡️` (link to another article), `🇺🇸 US-only feature:`. Emoji also appear in directory category chips and user-supplied display names — not Linktree's writing.
- **Links are descriptive anchors**, never bare URLs and never "click here": "See the full TikTok link in bio guide →", "Learn how to set up Linktree Shops", "Browse templates". Cross-link arrows use `→`. Catalog pages keep links as standalone labels on their own line.
- **Tables are narrow and purposeful** — 2–3 column comparison grids in blog and pricing (Linktree always the right-hand column, ending "Minutes" vs "Hours or days"), and fee matrices in help. No spec tables, no feature matrices elsewhere.
- **ALL CAPS has two jobs only**: section kickers and legally required disclosures in Terms (auto-renewal, pending earnings). Never for emphasis in prose.
- **Ampersand in names, titles, and tight pairs** ("Sell, share & earn", "Email & SMS Integrations"), never in body sentences.
- **Asterisked footnotes** carry every statistic and every limit, set immediately under the CTA, usually ending "See here for more details."
- **Bracketed placeholders** in template copy the reader is meant to fill: "[City]", "[ZIP code/suburb]", "[X]".

## Genre notes

The site splits into six registers that share one lexicon. They must not be blended.

1. **Product / marketing** (feature and solutions pages, pricing, the homepage) — the tightest voice. Eyebrow → headline → 20–40 word body → CTA, repeated. Zero emoji, zero bold, near-zero exclamation marks. Objections live only in the FAQ, phrased in the reader's "I".
2. **Blog, SEO long-form** — warm, exclamation-tolerant, objection-handling, bold-label bullets, comparison table, myth-buster section, one terminal CTA containing "free".
3. **Blog, data and editorial** — the counter-voice: short declaratives, deliberate fragments, no "we", no exclamation marks, stats stated with `~` and `×`, and a `Methodology:` footnote naming the window and formula.
4. **Help center** — task-shaped titles, bare imperatives, verdict-first FAQ answers ("Yes!", "Definitely not."), the emoji callout system, contractions intact in fee and payout copy.
5. **Policy and legal** — the marketing grammar with hard edges. First person and second person survive; question-form headings; ALL CAPS only where the law requires it. Terms explains its own tone: "One of our core values is to pursue deliberate simplicity."
6. **Creator Reports and Discover** — the loosest register. Reports are wry and censored-profane with a punch-then-explain rhythm; Discover intros are a fixed four-slot template full of "our curated collection" and a closing "Let's … together". The per-creator card blurbs on Discover are visibly machine-generated (*journey, delve, passionate, treasure trove*) and are **not** a model for the brand voice.

## Synthetic samples

Composed to demonstrate the register — not text from the site.

**1 — Product / marketing** (the atomic feature block)

> Schedule smarter
> Plan once. Post all week.
> Your queue publishes on autopilot while you make the next thing. Set your times in a few clicks and let it run — no spreadsheets, no reminders. That's hours back every week.

**2 — Blog, SEO long-form** (article opening)

> Posting every day sounds simple until you actually run a bakery. Between early ovens, custom orders, and delivery runs, your feed is the first thing to slip. And the longer it sits quiet, the harder it feels to start again.
> The good news? You don't need a marketing team. A handful of repeatable post ideas — and one page that holds everything you sell — can turn casual scrollers into regular customers.

**3 — Blog, data and editorial** (the counter-voice)

> Most profile visits last under a minute. The clicks show where that minute goes.
> Weekends behave differently. Visitors click ~2× more on a Saturday morning than on a weekday afternoon, and they go deeper — past the first link, into the catalog.
> Attention arrives in bursts. Pages built for the burst convert it. Pages that aren't watch it pass.

**4 — Help center** (task orientation, steps, completion line)

> Add a booking link to your page — whether you run a studio, a salon, or a one-person consultancy. Once it's live, visitors pick a time and you get the details, hands-free.
>
> 1. Open the editor and select **+ Add**.
> 2. Choose your booking tool and connect your calendar.
> 3. Set your available hours and select **Save**.
>
> Your booking link is now live!
>
> 💡 Tip: Add a short note about session length so visitors know exactly what they're reserving.

**5 — Policy and legal** (rules in the house voice)

> These guidelines exist so your page stays somewhere people can trust. They work alongside our Terms, and by keeping a page here you're agreeing to follow both — so take a few minutes with them.
> Most of it is common sense. Don't use your page to mislead people, impersonate someone else, or sell what you can't deliver. You may promote your own work; you may not run scams or spam.
> A few rules have no soft edges. Content that harms children is removed and reported, every time.

**6 — Creator report** (the loosest register)

> Nobody started making things because they love feeding an algorithm. The feed wants more, the metrics want whatever they want this week, and you just want to finish the thing you started.
> So here's the short version. Make what you'd make anyway. Charge what it's worth. Keep it somewhere one link can carry. The rest is noise — and noise doesn't pay rent.

## Rewrite instructions

**Preserve from the source, unchanged:** every fact, number, date, price, name, product name, API/CLI/technical identifier, quotation, and citation. Preserve the source's language — a Russian document stays Russian, with these habits applied to Russian prose. Preserve the structure of meaning: the same sections in the same order, the same claims in the same relationships. Preserve required legal or safety content verbatim; you may rephrase around it, never through it.

**Replace:** voice, rhythm, lexicon, and formatting.

1. **Flip to second person.** Find the sentences whose subject is the product, the company, or an abstract noun, and rewrite them so the reader is the subject or the owner. "The platform enables users to schedule posts" → "Schedule your posts across platforms."
2. **Cut every abstraction on the Don't list** and replace with a plain verb or a number. If an adjective is doing persuasion work, check whether a figure from the source can do it instead.
3. **Find the transformation.** Somewhere in each section there is an X the reader already has and a Y they want. State it as `turn X into Y` once per document — twice at most. Do not force it into every section.
4. **Break the paragraphs.** Nothing over three sentences. Insert a one-sentence paragraph at each turn in the argument.
5. **Re-cut the headings.** Body headings become verb-first benefits or the search question written out. Give the important ones the two-beat shape and a terminal period; leave noun-phrase headings without one.
6. **De-risk every promise** in the same sentence or the next: cost, cancellation, availability, or the limit. Push the fine print into an asterisked line rather than softening the claim itself.
7. **Attribute limits, never apologize for them.** Name the third party or the regulation, then give the path that still works.
8. **Strip the formatting.** Remove bold and italics from prose. In marketing-shaped text, convert bullet lists to short title-plus-sentence blocks; in article-shaped text, convert them to bold-label bullets under a colon stem; in procedural text, convert them to numbered bare imperatives. Remove all emoji unless the document is a help article, where the four-callout system (`📝 Note:`, `💡 Tip:`, `➡️`, geographic gate) is available.
9. **End on a CTA or a routing list, never a summary.** One CTA per document, terminal, imperative, 2–5 words, containing "free" when a free path exists. If the document has recurring CTAs, use one string and repeat it verbatim.
10. **Ration the exclamation marks** to affirmations, completion lines, and jokes — zero in feature or marketing bodies.

**How strictly to imitate the synthetic samples:** match sample 1 for any product or feature block, sample 2 for an article opening, sample 3 when the source is data-led, sample 4 for procedural documentation, and sample 5 for rules and policy. Match their *shape* — block length, sentence count, where the punch lands — not their words; lifting a phrase like "That's a win-win" more than once per document reads as pastiche. When the source genre has no match above, default to register 1 (product/marketing) and keep sentences under 25 words.

**One deliberate imperfection to allow, not chase:** the site's spelling, serial comma, and dash typography are genuinely inconsistent. In a rewrite, pick one convention per document and hold it — the inconsistency is documented here so it is recognized as authentic, not so it is reproduced.
