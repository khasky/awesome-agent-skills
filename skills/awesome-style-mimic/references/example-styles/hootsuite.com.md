# Style: hootsuite.com

Generated from 209 pages (100% of non-serial site, plus quota samples of 9 serial templates) on 2026-08-09.

## Voice profile

The author is a veteran social media manager who now writes for the vendor — someone who has personally
suffered the overflowing inbox, the 3pm approval scramble, and the boss who asks "so what did social
actually do for us?" They write to a peer under time pressure, never to an executive committee. Their
default sentence is a verb-first imperative aimed at `you`; `we` appears only when the brand supplies
something, reassures, or makes a joke at its own expense. They are confident to the point of bluntness —
verdicts land as bare fragments ("Period." "Hands down." "Hint: it's not Loomly.") with no hedging
vocabulary anywhere — but the confidence is always cashed out in a concrete number, a named customer, or a
named missing feature in a rival, never in adjectives. Humor is dry, rationed to roughly one beat per
page, delivered in a parenthetical or a final short clause, and aimed at the workload, the competitor's
name, or Hootsuite itself — never at the reader. Underneath the jokes the copy is unusually candid: plan
pages state who the plan is *not* for, FAQ answers admit limits with a bare "No.", and the paywall is
acknowledged with an apology-shaped joke rather than pressure.

## Tone rules

**Do**

- Address `you` and skip the subject: "Track mentions, monitor sentiment, and benchmark against competitors."
- State claims flatly, then immediately prove them with a numeral, a named customer, or a named capability.
- Validate the reader's grievance before selling: open with two or three clipped "You've …" sentences naming what the reader has already tried and watched fail, then pivot.
- Name the enemy as workload, tabs, spreadsheets, the algorithm, or "the robots" — take the reader's side against it.
- Close a capability sentence with its payoff: `so you can …`, `giving you …`, `helping you …`.
- Use contractions everywhere (you'll, it's, don't, we've, isn't, here's), including in headings.
- Break rhythm with a 2–5 word fragment: "Only in Hootsuite." "Yup, just one." "Until now." "Win-win."
- Admit limits plainly and route the reader somewhere — a reassuring "don't worry" beat, then a pointer to the plan or tool that does fit.
- Keep one joke per page, in an aside or a kicker, then move on in the next sentence.

**Don't**

- Don't hedge: no *arguably, perhaps, we believe, in our opinion, tends to, it's worth noting, that said*.
- Don't use academic connectors: *furthermore, moreover, additionally, in conclusion, however* (rare at best).
- Don't use consultant abstractions: *synergy, paradigm, holistic, ecosystem, best-of-breed, digital transformation, delve, unpack, navigate the landscape, journey, at the end of the day, in today's fast-paced world*.
- Don't write mission-statement grandiosity — no "our mission is", no "we believe" outside the ESG/social-impact register.
- Don't use *utilize*; *leverage / robust / seamless / empower* appear at most once and only glued to a concrete noun.
- Don't apologize or sympathize in corporate register ("we understand that…", "unfortunately") — except as a joke.
- Don't sneer at competitors; the insult is always a specific missing feature, and a compliment usually precedes the pivot.
- Don't put exclamation marks in hero copy or H1s — they belong in FAQ answers and testimonials only.
- Don't use emoji except as documented below (near-zero: ~10 glyphs across 209 pages).
- Don't spell out numbers; don't bold keywords mid-sentence; don't stack subordinate clauses.

## Lexicon

**Signature tic — "social" as a bare mass noun.** "on social", "across social", "win on social",
"results on social", "the power of social", "The answer's on social", "social moves fast". Never "socials";
never "social platforms" when "social" alone will do. This one habit does more to sound like Hootsuite than
anything else in this document.

**The one-place cluster** (the central product claim, ~10 surface forms): in one place, all in one tab,
one dashboard, one inbox, a single inbox, one calendar, one connected suite, one central hub, side by side,
without switching tools, without ever leaving the Hootsuite dashboard. Its negative twin is equally fixed:
"No more ping-ponging between tabs", "Go ahead and close those tabs", "hop-scotching between a dozen open
tabs", "no tab switching required". Recurring footer line: "A dozen social media tools. One tab."

**Speed and effort:** in seconds, in minutes, in a few clicks, in one click, right out of the box, at your
fingertips, a breeze, easy-peasy, a cinch, save time (and your sanity), the heavy lifting, busywork,
without breaking a sweat, in half the time.

**Verbs of ascent and combat:** grow, boost, skyrocket, unlock, level up, scale, outpace, outperform,
outshine, beat, crush, dazzle, delight, capitalize on, stay ahead, get ahead, spot opportunities sooner,
prove your impact, prove the value of your hard work.

**"Actually" as the credibility marker:** "content your audience actually wants", "Make benchmarking
actually painless", "Intelligence that actually moves the needle", "showing how each one actually works",
"the metrics that really matter".

**Owl / nest motif** (light, never labored): soar, the iconic red owl, Owly, Ow.ly, OwlyWriter AI,
HootGiving, HootPartner, "a birds-eye view", "Beyond the Nest". Every new-brand product page closes with a
"Why is it named X?" FAQ answered through owl lore ("A parliament is the collective noun for a group of owls").

**Product nouns are proper nouns**, capitalized mid-sentence, never explained twice: Wisdom (the AI
teammate), Perch (publishing), Nest (inbox), Lumen (listening), Parliament (advocacy, formerly Amplify),
Social OS, Composer, Planner, Streams, Analytics, Inbox, Blue Silk™ AI, Social Content Ratings®, Best Time
to Publish, Talkwalker.

**Proof nouns used as vocabulary:** 150 million websites, 30+ social networks, 300+ review sites, 187
languages, 100+ integrations, 200,000+ paid users, 22+ million users, 175+ countries, 600+ of the Fortune
1000, five-star reviews on G2, FedRAMP, HIPAA-friendly, SOC 2, GDPR/CCPA/PIPEDA.

**Verbatim reassurance formulas** (reuse these exact strings): "No credit card required. Cancel anytime.",
"No strings attached.", "risk-free trial or demo", "zero-risk trial".

**Colloquial register words** a stiffer brand would cut: whip up, a ton of, way more, yep, yup, psst,
handy-dandy, back-pocket hacks, savvy, faves, must-have, inspo, a whopping, duh, slays, frenemies, moola,
scroll-stopping, "go take a nap", "take the dread out of", "big kahunas", "you gotta".

**Known inconsistencies in the source — pick one and hold it.** The advocacy product is called both
Amplify and Parliament (sometimes in one sentence); the AI writer is called both OwlyWriter AI and Wisdom;
the rank claim is spelled "no. 1", "#1", "number one" and "most trusted"; em dashes are spaced on newer
pages and unspaced on older ones; British spellings (favourite, behaviour, customise) leak into a minority
of pages. Choose the newer convention (Parliament, Wisdom, "no. 1" for self-claims and "#1" for third-party
awards, spaced em dash, US spelling) and apply it consistently.

## Rhythm & syntax

- **Sentences run 8–22 words.** Hero subheads 15–25. FAQ answers 30–90 words split into 1–3 paragraphs.
  Long constructions are lists, never subordination; almost no sentence carries more than one dependent clause.
- **Paragraphs are 1–3 sentences.** The default body block is exactly two: sentence 1 is the payoff,
  sentence 2 is the mechanism.
- **"Plus," sentence-initial is the workhorse connector** — dozens of instances sitewide. "Then", "And",
  "But", "So", "With Hootsuite,", "Unlike X," also open sentences. Formal conjunctive adverbs are absent.
- **Rule of three is the default cadence:** "Publish directly, schedule posts, track conversations";
  "Happier customers. Lighter workload. Bigger impact."; "spot opportunities sooner, respond faster, and
  stay ahead". Verb series of 3–6 with the serial comma is the standard sentence engine.
- **The spaced em dash ( — ) is the signature mark**, in three jobs: the appositive gloss ("with the
  planner — the calendar built for busy social teams"), the payoff tail appended to a complete
  sentence ("— all without digging through dashboards", "— minus the risk"), and the interruption for a joke.
  Two to four per page.
- **Fragments punctuate.** "Only in Hootsuite." "Period." "Hands down." "All in one tab." "Very easy."
  "Your competition could never." "Finally."
- **Question → answer is the second engine.** A rhetorical question as its own paragraph or heading,
  answered in the next line: "But is it really that important? Well, yes." / "The catch?" / "The challenge?"
  / "What's a strategist to do?" / "They asked: How do we…? The answer: Hootsuite Enterprise."
- **Antithesis in two beats:** "Most teams collect more dashboards. Few open them twice." / "Surveys tell
  you what people claim. Comments show you what they mean." — beat one names the crowd, beat two the exception.
- **Parentheses carry the second voice** — the joke, the second promise, or the scope caveat:
  "(and keep customers happy)", "(and prove your impact)", "(Enterprise plan only)", "(Hootsuite, duh!)".
- **Colon sets up reveals and labels:** "Hint:", "Spoiler alert:", "Side note:", "Translation:", "Step 1:",
  "Or worse: type it out by hand."
- **Numerals always**, never spelled out: 46%, 3X, 10x, 750%, $42.7m, 200K+, 3,000 hours, 240+. Floors take
  a "+". Lowercase "m" for millions in money figures.
- **Ellipsis for the comic pause:** "Drum roll, please… $0."
- No semicolons in body copy (one rare exception). Sentence-case headings.

## Structure

**The universal landing / product skeleton** (deviate only with reason):

1. H1 = a benefit promise, never a bare noun. Two shapes: `<Product>: <verb phrase>` ("Perch: Create weeks
   of content in minutes") or `<Verb> <outcome> with <named thing>` ("Boost reach with an easy-peasy
   Pinterest scheduler").
2. One- or two-sentence deck restating the promise and naming the mechanism.
3. CTA pair, adjacent: "Start your free trial" + "Request a demo" (demo-only on enterprise pages).
4. The reassurance line, verbatim: "No credit card required. Cancel anytime."
5. Social-proof band: a logo-wall line or three stat tiles.
6. A benefit triad with one-word labels (Trim / Track / Analyze; Brainstorm / Draft / Schedule).
7. 3–6 benefit blocks: outcome heading (a full sentence with attitude, not a label) + 25–60 word body.
8. Named-customer pull quote — quote, then Name, Title, Company.
9. Feature grid of 6–10 cards: noun-phrase title + 1–2 sentences.
10. Cross-sell module ("Everything social, all in one suite.") and resource cards with `Read more →`.
11. FAQ, 8–14 questions.
12. Closing CTA band: a pun or rhetorical-question headline + one reassuring sentence + the same two buttons.

**FAQ convention.** Question as heading; the answer opens with a bare verdict word — "Yes." / "Yep!" /
"Absolutely!" / "No." / "Not yet, but it's coming soon!" — then 1–3 short paragraphs, and closes by routing
somewhere ("Learn more.", "Compare all Hootsuite plans.", "Get in touch!"). Negative answers stay upbeat and
never dead-end. Plan pages always carry the paired "Who is X good for?" / "Who is X **not** good for?".

**Case study skeleton:** industry eyebrow → question or "How [X] [verbed] [number]" H1 → CTA pair → exactly
three stat tiles → THE CHALLENGE / THE SOLUTION / THE RESULTS (or "Who they are" / "How they did it" /
"What's next") → constraint → the customer's question in their own voice → "The answer: Hootsuite …" →
concrete anecdote → pull quote → the fixed closing block.

**Release note ("What's New") skeleton:** one-sentence deck with the feature name as grammatical subject and
a `so you can` tail → `Details` containing a `Previously, … / Now (or With X), …` pivot → `Key Benefits` as
`imperative label: one sentence.` bullets (three of them) → `How it Works` opening "Setting up X is simple:"
→ a mirror-close restating the deck → "Learn more in the Help Center."

**Press release skeleton:** eyebrow → subhead → city/date dateline with an inline appositive → a stat →
paired executive quotes (attribution after the first sentence: `said Name, Title, Hootsuite`) → About
boilerplate → a fixed closing sentence inviting readers to visit the company website. Third person
throughout; "we" only inside quotes.

**Webinar / event skeleton:** category → colon-split title → deck with the two-beat antithesis → "What
you'll learn" bullets phrased as `How to …` → Details ending in a `Join <named exec> …` invitation →
speakers → three related cards → the global CTA.

**Closing headlines are a rhetorical question or an imperative promise**, often punning on the competitor
or the topic: "Ready to grow your business on Facebook?", "Why sprout when you can blossom?", "You're one
click away from becoming a link legend", "Put social media to work in every corner of your business".

## Formatting habits

- **Bullets** are verb-first fragments, parallel, no terminal punctuation, 3–6 items, introduced by a colon
  lead-in and closed with an escape hatch: "And much more." FAQ bullets are full sentences with periods.
- **Bold is a run-in head**, never mid-sentence emphasis: a bolded promise glued to an unbolded mechanism
  sentence. Release-note bullets use `**Imperative label:** one sentence.`
- **No italics in body prose.** Emphasis comes from fragments, headings, and stat tiles. ALL-CAPS only
  inside testimonials ("saves me time EVERY DAY!") or on the loudest self-serve pages.
- **Links** are phrase-anchored and imperative: "Learn more", "Read the case study", "Compare all Hootsuite
  plans", "Here's how.", "Get in touch!" — never a bare URL. Secondary/card links append ` →`; primary CTAs
  don't.
- **Emoji are effectively banned** — roughly ten glyphs across 209 pages, and always zoned: 🥰 on the
  "our customers love us" stat, 😉 in one FAQ aside, ✨ before "Pro tip:", 😱 in one Ow.ly FAQ, and a mirrored
  mood row (🙁 💸 😵 / 🙂 📈 🧘) on the "why us" page. Zero emoji in hero copy, About, DEI, research, case
  studies, release notes, press releases, and webinars. The house move is to *name* an emoji instead of using
  one: "Insert melty face emoji here."
- **Stat tiles** render as a bare number then a sentence naming the customer and the mechanism, shaped
  `<N>% — <Customer> grew <metric> by <N>% using <product> to <mechanism>…` — the number repeats inside the sentence.
- **Footnote system** is consistent: `Yes*` resolves to `*Enterprise plan only`; pricing carries
  `*Prices displayed in USD, based on annual billing…`; comparison tables carry `Last updated <date>`.
- **Trademark discipline:** `Blue Silk™ AI`, `Social Content Ratings®`; platform dual-naming `X (Twitter)`.
- **Comparison tables** are three columns with values limited to Yes / No / Yes* / Limited / a short qualifier.
- **Repetition is a convention, not a bug.** Summary blurbs, testimonials, FAQ answers, feature tiles and the
  closing CTA band are reused word-for-word across sibling pages. When writing a family of pages, clone the
  fixed blocks deliberately.

## Genre notes

The site is one brand in **five registers**. Never blend them on a single page.

1. **Self-serve / free-tool / SMB** (the free-tools family, Ow.ly, Pinterest, Standard plan) — the highest
   joke density. Peer-collapsing "we" ("Yeah, us too", "As marketers just like you"), the algorithm and "the
   robots" as recurring characters, cooking metaphors (magic, secret sauce, whip up), a long setup that
   indicts the reader's workload followed by a "But" pivot to the free tool. Step-by-step instructions in
   `Step N:` blocks with permission to be lazy ("No need to get super-specific").
2. **Enterprise / platform / use-case** (platform and use-case pages, listening, crisis, reputation) —
   sober, stat-driven, demo-only CTAs, imperative verb triads, no slang, no jokes. Proof by scale and named
   Fortune-1000 customers.
3. **Editorial / research / case study** (research reports and the resource library) — "we did the research" authority,
   the reader's boss as a character ("impress your boss", "Takeaways for your leadership team"), one slang
   island per page, question→answer construction, anecdote as proof (the specific cocktail, the plush
   mascots) instead of a benefits list.
4. **Corporate / press / ESG** (newsroom, social impact, DEI, legal pages) — third person, "we" only
   inside quotation marks, no contractions on ESG pages, zero humor, fixed boilerplate strings ("unlock the
   value of their social media relationships", the closing invitation to visit the company website). Aspirational verbs
   (strive, pledge, aim) replace competitive ones.
5. **Release notes / What's New** — the feature is the grammatical subject, no first person, no stats, no
   customer proof, no jokes, one exclamation mark across the whole genre, rigid template slots.

Webinar pages sit between 2 and 3: sober, no proof numbers, no competitor named — the antagonist is time
("before the market moves", "before it hits the shelf"), and authority comes from named speakers.

## Rewrite instructions

**Preserve from the source document:** every fact, number, date, name, product term, code sample, link
target, and claim; the source language; the order of meaning (what argument comes before what); any legal,
safety, or compliance wording. Never invent a statistic, customer, or award to make a passage sound more
like Hootsuite — the brand's confidence is always backed by a real number, so if the source has none, use a
concrete capability instead.

**Replace:** voice, sentence length, connectors, heading wording, list grammar, and formatting habits.

**Method:**

1. **Pick the register first** from the five in *Genre notes*. A README or release note takes register 5; a
   comparison or product page takes register 1 or 2 depending on the buyer; an announcement takes 4. Applying
   the free-tool joke register to enterprise or ESG content is the single most common way to get this wrong.
2. **Rewrite the opening as a promise, not a description.** Turn the source's topic sentence into either a
   `<Verb> <outcome> with <thing>` headline or a two-beat antithesis, and follow it with one sentence naming
   the mechanism.
3. **Cut every sentence to 8–22 words** and split paragraphs to 1–3 sentences with the payoff/mechanism
   pairing. Where the source stacks clauses, break them into a verb triad.
4. **Convert benefits to second person** and append a `so you can …` tail to capability statements.
5. **Insert the house connectors**: start sentences with "Plus," / "Then" / "But" / "With X,"; delete
   *furthermore, moreover, additionally, however*.
6. **Add exactly one fragment beat** per section and, in registers 1 and 3 only, exactly one dry joke in a
   parenthetical or a closing clause.
7. **Add one rhetorical question** used as a pivot, answered in the next line.
8. **Reformat lists** to verb-first fragments without terminal punctuation, three to six items, closing with
   "And much more." where a list is obviously partial.
9. **Strip all hedging and consultant vocabulary** listed in *Tone rules*; convert spelled-out numbers to
   numerals; make em dashes spaced.
10. **Close with a rhetorical-question or imperative CTA headline** plus a reassurance line — reuse "No
    credit card required. Cancel anytime." verbatim only when a free trial genuinely applies.

**Imitation strictness:** copy the *structural* patterns (skeletons, bullet grammar, FAQ verdict openers,
the `Previously … / Now …` pivot, the `so you can` tail) closely — they are templated on the real site and
reusing them is idiomatic, not plagiarism. Copy the *sentences* of the synthetic samples never; they are voice
calibration, not phrasing to lift. The fixed strings that SHOULD be reused verbatim are only these: "No
credit card required. Cancel anytime.", "No strings attached.", "And much more.", "Everything social, all in
one suite.", "Learn more in the Help Center." — plus, in the corporate register, the fixed closing sentence
that invites readers to visit the company website.
