# Style: zapier.com

Generated from 209 pages (100% of non-serial site, 141/141 pages; serial sections sampled) on 2026-08-09.

Crawled sections: homepage and product landings, `/solutions/*`, the blog (roundups, long-form explainers, editorial), customer stories, `/resources/*` guides and reports, playbooks, events and webinars, developer platform, careers and values, press, pricing, security, legal. Six programmatic sections — `/automation/*`, `/automations/*`, `/templates/details/*`, `/apps/*/integrations/*`, `/find-apps/*`, `/partnerdirectory/*` — were sampled and are **excluded as anti-models** (see Genre notes).

---

## Voice profile

Zapier writes as a competent colleague who has already done the boring part for you. The reader is always "you" — an operator with a job and no patience — and Zapier itself is usually the third-person instrument in the sentence, not the hero: *Zapier handles the keys*, *Let Zapier do the admin work*. The persuasion is structural, not adjectival: name the drudgery in concrete nouns, state the mechanism in one clause, land on what the reader gets back. Where another SaaS site reaches for an adjective, Zapier reaches for a number.

Two registers sit under one voice. Marketing pages are warm but pronoun-disciplined: no "I", one dry line per section, never a joke for its own sake. The blog is the opposite — a named human with a byline, real opinions, an off-topic personal anecdote before any product word, and a willingness to say a famous product is mediocre. Both share the same trust device: credibility bought with admissions rather than claims ("We know we'll make mistakes", "We're never paid for placement", a cons list that names real drawbacks).

The single strongest fingerprint is negation-then-correction — assert the wrong frame, then swap it: *isn't magic—it's math*, *IT sets boundaries, not bottlenecks*, *Designed for signal, not scale.* It shows up in landing copy, playbooks, and the blog alike.

## Tone rules

**Do**

- Make "you" the subject of every benefit line; refer to Zapier in the third person on marketing pages.
- End feature sentences on the reader's payoff, usually via a `so you can` / `so <role> can` clause.
- Name the drudgery in concrete nouns before naming the fix — "the pre-call research, activity logging, and order processing".
- Use negation-then-correction to reframe: *not X — Y*.
- Concede the reader's objection in two words ("You could."), then price out the do-it-yourself route as a list of the maintenance chores it actually costs.
- Attach a number instead of an adjective: 9,000+ apps, 3.4 million companies, 6 mins to set up a Zap.
- Define your own jargon inline on first use — "Zaps," which are automated workflows".
- Answer FAQs with the verdict first: "Absolutely." / "No coding required." / "Most likely, yes."
- State drawbacks plainly, including your own product's ("A bit buggy", "known gaps we're actively closing").
- Use contractions everywhere except legal.
- Close every page by restating its own headline, shorter, above a single button.

**Don't**

- Don't use "I" on a page with no byline.
- Don't let a superlative do work a number could do.
- Don't write a jokey aside on a landing page — one dry clause per section is the ceiling.
- Don't use "click here", bare URLs, or generic link text in body copy.
- Don't use emoji. Not one, in any genre.
- Don't stack exclamation marks; the whole site runs on roughly zero.
- Don't hedge with "arguably", "it's worth noting", "in today's fast-paced world".
- Don't write the templated closer — "The result is A, B, and C." / "Users gain…" — that is the machine register, not the house voice.
- Don't Title Case a heading; sentence case throughout.

## Lexicon

**Product nouns**, capitalized, treated as proper nouns and never re-explained: Zap, Zaps, Zap Templates, Zapier Tables, Interfaces, Chatbots, Canvas, Copilot, Agents, Zapier MCP, Zapier SDK, Lead Router, Paths, Filters, Digests, Sub-Zaps. The naming pattern for built-in steps is fixed: **`<Thing> by Zapier`** — Code by Zapier, Formatter by Zapier, Webhooks by Zapier, Schedule by Zapier, AI by Zapier.

**Mechanics vocabulary**, always glossed on first use: trigger, action, step, task, Polling / Instant, Write / Search, tool call, agentic workflow. "A trigger is an event that starts a workflow, and an action is an event a Zap performs."

**Workhorse verbs:** automate, connect, sync, trigger, route, capture, streamline, eliminate, surface, centralize, unify, orchestrate, hand off, free up, scale.

**The enemy has fixed names:** busy work, manual data entry, manual effort, admin work, grunt work, "slip through the cracks", "manual and error-prone", "time-in-seat".

**House idiom** (highest-density on the press and values pages): "work with what you work with", "make magic happen", "gets stuff done", "moving the needle", "single source of truth", "tech stack", "no code required", "without writing a single line of code", "in minutes, not months".

**2026 AI-era layer:** AI agents, orchestration, guardrails, governance, audit trail, observability, model access, BYOM, shadow IT, AI fluency, Golden Paths, "pilot to production", "delegate real work to AI".

**Proof numerals, reused verbatim:** 9,000+ apps · 3.4 million companies · 25M–27M Zaps · 800+ people across 40 countries · 93% of customers say Zapier made them better at their job · 6 mins average Zap setup. Always digits, thousands separated, `+` for a floor. *(The live site drifts between 7,000+/8,000+/9,000+/10,000+ apps and 3.4M/3.7M/2.2M companies. That is a defect, not a style — pick one number and hold it.)*

**Fixed CTA strings:** "Get started free" · "Start free with email" · "Continue with Google" · "Try it" · "Try for free" · "Contact Sales" · "Learn more" · "Use this template" · "See how Zapier works".

**Recurring closers:** "See what automation can do for your business." · a two-sentence productivity promise (imperative benefit line, then an invitation to get your apps working together) · "Join forward-thinking enterprises that trust Zapier for mission-critical workflows."

**Words this author never uses:** revolutionary, game-changing, cutting-edge, best-in-class, world-class, next-generation, synergy, paradigm, delve, utilize, harness, supercharge, elevate, embark, journey, "in today's fast-paced world", "let's dive in", "TL;DR", "solutions" as a vague noun.

**Words that mark copy as templated or legacy — avoid them:** *seamless / seamlessly*, *leverage*, *unlock*, *empower*, *effortlessly*, *robust*. These appear on the site only in programmatic pages, vendor blurbs, and older `/solutions/*` copy — never in 2026 first-party prose. They are the cheapest authored-vs-generated detector on the whole domain.

## Rhythm & syntax

- **Sentences: 8–25 words** on marketing pages, median around 15. Blog sentences average 20–25 with hard swings.
- **Paragraphs: 1–4 sentences** on marketing pages, 2–5 in the blog. The marketing unit is a **three-sentence block**: friction/context → mechanism → outcome.
- **Fragments carry the emphasis beats**, three to eight words, usually after a long sentence: "Your tools. Your rules. Any AI." / "One configuration, no exceptions." / "Bad data makes bad AI." / "It's worth it."
- **Signature move 1 — negation-then-correction.** "AI transformation at scale isn't magic—it's math." / "That's not a technology gap—it's a leadership gap." / "IT sets boundaries, not bottlenecks."
- **Signature move 2 — the purpose clause.** Feature, em dash or "so", reader outcome: name the feature plainly, gloss the drudgery it absorbs as a three-noun appositive between dashes, and land on a `so <role> can` clause that states the reader's win.
- **Signature move 3 — concede, then price it out.** A two-word concession ("You could."), then the do-it-yourself route invoiced as a comma-chained list of maintenance chores, trailing off in an ellipsis.
- **Signature move 4 — "That's where X comes in"** after a rhetorical question naming the reader's bandwidth problem.
- **Signature move 5 — the self-puncturing aside** (blog only): undercut your own advice in a parenthetical rather than posing as the expert who already does everything right.
- **Rule of three is the house cadence:** "speed, visibility, and control" · "cleaner data, faster processes, and better visibility" · "Connect any AI. Govern every team. See everything."
- **Em dash is the house connector** — for the appositive gloss, the aside, and the reversal. Older copy sets it **unspaced** (`everything—giving you`); 2026 landing and security copy sets it **spaced** (`see everything — so everyone can build`). Pick one per document and hold it; the spaced form is the newer habit.
- **Colons introduce payoffs and lists**, usually after "Here's": "Here's the key:", "here's what a low match rate is actually costing you:".
- **Rhetorical questions open sections** and are answered immediately, often with a bare verdict.
- Semicolons are effectively absent outside pricing lines and legal. Oxford comma always.
- **Scare quotes hold borrowed jargon at arm's length:** "manager of one", "good", "husky".
- **Numerals for everything** — quantities, prices, percentages; `$12/month (billed annually)`, `$16.80/user/month`, `9-to-5`, `24/7`. Numbers under ten are spelled out in running prose but not in headings.
- **Legal inverts all of it:** 60–120-word sentences, roman-numeral enumeration, ALL-CAPS liability blocks, zero contractions — with one human preamble line ("Thanks for using Zapier!").

## Structure

**Product / solutions landing** — the most repeated skeleton on the site:

1. Small kicker label ("Enterprise security", "Zapier for Sales", "For builders").
2. Benefit headline: a fragment or a short clause, sentence case.
3. One-sentence deck naming the mechanism, not the benefit twice.
4. Paired CTAs — a self-serve path and a sales path ("Start free with email" + "Contact Sales").
5. Trust strip: "Trusted by 3.4 million companies".
6. Three benefit blocks, each a **label + one-sentence definition**.
7. Tabbed use cases, then a named customer quote, then a template grid.
8. Three differentiators (ease of use / customizability / security).
9. FAQ, questions phrased in the reader's own words, answers opening on a bare verdict.
10. Closing CTA that restates the headline shorter.

**Blog roundup ("The best X in <year>")** — the most codified template in the corpus: breadcrumb → read time → H1 with the year → one-line dek → byline + date → affiliate disclosure → three-paragraph intro (category state → personal hook → scale of testing) → the picks as "**App** for **use case**" → "What makes the best X?" criteria with bold lead-in bullets → the boxed **"How we evaluate and test apps"** trust statement → at-a-glance table → per-app block (`Best X for Y` / app name with platforms in parentheses / pros / cons / 3–5 paragraphs / a Zapier-integration paragraph with template cards / `App price:` line / an alternative recommendation) → objection section ("What about the old standbys?") → "Related reading:" → newsletter box → **update-provenance line** ("This post was originally published in <year> and has since been updated…") → third-person author bio ending on a personal joke.

**Long-form blog explainer:** definition abstract → personal anecdote lede (two paragraphs, no product word) → "This guide will teach you everything you need to know about X." → table of contents → "What is X?" → "How X works" → "X vs. related concepts" → benefits → examples → "N X best practices" as numbered imperative headings → FAQ → "Related reading:" → update stamp.

**Customer story:** `How <Company> <verbs> with Zapier` → three metric tiles → Company Size / Company Industry → per-initiative **Challenge / Solution / Results** triads → attributed pull quote → "See what automation can do for your business" → "Contact Sales". Results paragraphs end on a scaling claim ("without increasing headcount").

**Playbook / research report:** chapter nav → "Why this matters" + a stat pair → diagnosis narrative → a named maturity ladder (Mobilize / Activate / Amplify / Sustain) → executive pull quotes → **self-assessment checklist of yes/no questions** → a scripted next step ("If you answered no to two or more, book time with…").

**Careers / values:** numbered imperative value names ("1. Default to action", "5. Build the robot") → two or three rationale paragraphs including a **mandatory self-qualifier** ("However: action is not an achievement unto itself.") → "Tips on how to apply (and not apply) this value:" → a do/don't table of first-person "I" statements.

**Webinar / event:** kicker → title → date and time → problem-first paragraph → speaker cards → "What you'll do" → "What you'll leave with" → "Who it's for" → objection handler ("Can't attend live?") → "Register now".

## Formatting habits

- **Zero emoji.** Verified across all 209 pages, every genre. The `→` glyph appears rarely, in promo banners and CTA text.
- **Sentence case for every heading and CTA.** Title Case headings are a programmatic-page tell.
- **Two bullet shapes.** Blog: **bold lead-in term, period, full explanatory sentence** — e.g. "**Offline mode.** The best tools in this category keep working when the connection drops…". Landing: `Label` + one-sentence definition, or a `Term — explanation` pair with an em dash.
- **Pros/cons bullets are bare noun fragments** with no terminal punctuation: "Excellent free plan", "Steep learning curve".
- **Bold marks list lead-ins, product names and template names — not emphasis inside sentences.** Emphasis comes from structure: tile titles, kickers, short paragraphs. The blog occasionally uses ALL CAPS as a joke ("ALL THE THINGS").
- **Links are descriptive anchors**, never "click here" and never bare URLs. The blog links out to competitors and to its own comparison posts freely.
- **Stats render as a numeral tile over a one-line gloss:** "93% / Customers who say using Zapier has made them better at their job".
- **Platform availability in parentheses after an app name:** `Canva (Web, Windows, Mac, Android, iOS)`.
- **Pricing as a semicolon-joined pair:** free tier; paid tier and what it unlocks.
- **Tables are rare** — the blog at-a-glance grid (Best for / Standout feature / Pricing) and the values do/don't table.
- **`Tip:` is the inline callout label** in blog best-practice sections.
- Pull quotes are attributed on a following line: "— Name, Title at Company". Bracketed editorial insertions inside customer quotes are standard: "[With automation], Support now has…".

## Genre notes

**Landing / solutions** — the default register. Second person, Zapier third person, no "I", one dry clause per section, fixed spine.

**Blog** — the only place "I" is licensed. Named byline, anecdote lede as an enforced house pattern (it appears across staff and contract writers alike), real opinions including negative verdicts on famous products, jokes in parentheses after the technical claim has landed, and an editorial-trust box that states the site is never paid for placement.

**Customer stories** — third person about the customer, Zapier as instrument; first person only inside pull quotes. The Challenge / Solution / Results triplet is portable and reused in playbooks and internal case studies.

**Playbooks / reports** — first-person plural and confessional about Zapier's own missteps, hedged, footnoted, "you" mostly dropped in favour of analyst-register aphorisms.

**Careers / values** — the warmest register. Promissory, letter-like, self-deprecating about Zapier's own automation (the careers pages joke that hiring review is one process the company refuses to automate), and the only place exclamation marks cluster.

**Legal** — a genuine register break: defined capitalized parties, "will" rather than "shall", ALL-CAPS liability, no contractions, no reader warmth. Do not let its formality leak elsewhere.

**Excluded as anti-models.** These are Zapier-published but not Zapier-authored, and imitating them produces exactly the generic copy the house voice avoids:

- `/automation/*` and `/automations/*` — machine-generated category and use-case hubs. Tells: an invariant three-sentence mold, "The result is A, B, and C." as almost every closer, third-person "Users gain…" / "Teams gain…" replacing "you", Title Case two-word benefit tiles, no contractions, no author, and visible slot leakage (a student-enrollment page that says "every buyer").
- `/templates/details/*` — templated marketing copy that imitates the blog voice with the specificity removed ("Why our X is a game-changer", "Absolutely!"), sometimes citing retired products.
- `/apps/*/integrations/*` — mostly rendered API metadata plus partner-authored trigger/action descriptions and vendor "About <App>" blurbs; FAQs on these pages read LLM-written and drift to "our platform" / "automation tools like ours".
- `/find-apps/*` and `/apps/categories/*` — generated indexes and vendor-supplied app blurbs; only the page furniture is Zapier's.
- `/partnerdirectory/*` — partner- and client-written copy, tonally the inverse of the brand (ALL-CAPS headers, emoji bullets, *leverage* / *utilize* / *world-class*).
- All testimonials and pull quotes anywhere on the site are customer voice, not Zapier's.

**Corpus defects worth not reproducing:** app-count and company-count claims drift between pages; `/l/partners` ships lorem placeholder text live; several pages carry carousel-duplicated paragraphs (a render artifact, not deliberate repetition).

## Synthetic samples

Composed to demonstrate the register — not text from the site.

**1 — Landing / solutions**

> Stop being the integration
>
> Your week disappears into copy-paste: order numbers into a spreadsheet, spreadsheet rows into email, email threads into the project board. Connect those tools once and the handoffs run themselves — so you can spend the afternoon on customers instead of clipboard duty. Automation isn't a project. It's a setting you flip. No code required.

**2 — Blog**

> I once color-coded my entire pantry. Labels, matching jars, a spreadsheet of expiration dates. The system lasted 11 days, because it depended on one person — me — remembering to update it after every grocery run. (I did not.) Most workflow tools fail the same way: they're organized, not automated, and organized collapses the first busy week. The fix isn't more discipline. It's taking yourself out of the loop, so the spreadsheet updates whether you remember it exists or not.

**3 — Customer story**

> A 12-person support team at a mid-size retailer rekeyed every product return by hand: order lookup, refund form, warehouse email, spreadsheet log. Four systems, one keyboard. Wiring the four together turned that chain into a single approval, and per-return handling fell from 9 minutes to about 90 seconds. The busiest quarter of the year went through without added headcount — and the spreadsheet nobody trusted became the audit log everybody checks.

**4 — Playbook / report**

> Most teams don't have an automation problem. They have an inventory problem: nobody can list which workflows exist, who owns them, or what breaks when one quietly stops. We learned this the slow way — our own first audit turned up roughly twice as many workflows as anyone had guessed. Before scaling anything, count everything. Ask three questions of each workflow: does a named person own it, does it log what it did, and would anyone notice within a day if it failed? If two answers are no, fix the foundations before the roadmap. Governance isn't a tax on speed — it's the reason speed survives an audit.

**5 — Careers / values**

> Applying anywhere means writing into silence, and most companies leave you there. We'd rather not. A person reads every application, and every applicant gets an answer — yes, no, or not yet — within two weeks. However: an answer is not the same thing as a match. Most people we admire won't fit the role that happens to be open, and we'll say so plainly instead of warmly and vaguely. If that sounds like a team you'd argue well with, we'd love to hear from you!

**6 — Legal**

> Thanks for reading — the friendly part ends here. The Customer grants the Provider a limited, non-exclusive, non-transferable right to process Customer Data solely to deliver the Services described in Section 2, provided that (i) processing complies with the Data Processing Addendum, (ii) access is limited to personnel bound by written confidentiality obligations, and (iii) records of processing are retained for the period stated in Section 9. This right will terminate upon expiry of the Agreement. THE SERVICES ARE PROVIDED "AS IS," AND THE PROVIDER DISCLAIMS ALL WARRANTIES NOT EXPRESSLY STATED IN THIS AGREEMENT.

## Rewrite instructions

**Preserve from the source document, unchanged:**

- Every fact, figure, date, name, product name, URL, command, and code sample. Never invent a statistic to satisfy the "attach a number" habit — if the source has no number, restructure the sentence.
- The source language. This style is defined by rhythm, structure, and lexicon; it applies in whatever language the source is written in.
- The document's argument order and its section boundaries. Rephrase headings into this voice; do not merge, reorder, or drop sections.
- Legal, safety, and compliance wording, verbatim.

**Replace:**

- **Voice.** Convert third person and passive constructions to second person with the reader as subject. On marketing-style documents, refer to the product or company in the third person and reserve "we" for promises, decisions, and limits the organization is owning. Use "I" only if the source document has a named author.
- **Rhythm.** Break long sentences at their natural pivot; target 8–25 words. Split any paragraph over four sentences. After a long explanatory sentence, promote or add a short fragment beat — but only where the source already implies that conclusion.
- **Lexicon.** Delete every banned word listed above and substitute the plain equivalent (use, not utilize; use, not leverage). Delete *seamless*, *unlock*, *empower*, *effortlessly*, *robust* outright — on this site they mark generated copy. Replace vague evaluative adjectives with a figure the source already contains.
- **Openings.** Replace throat-clearing preambles with one of the house openers: the reader's friction named in concrete nouns, a rhetorical question about their bandwidth, a definition sentence, or — for editorial pieces only — a specific personal anecdote that carries the thesis before the thesis is stated.
- **Closings.** End by restating the document's own promise, shorter, above one action. For editorial pieces, close on a verdict or a joke, not a summary.
- **Formatting.** Convert emphasis-bold in body prose to sentence structure. Convert emoji bullets to plain bullets and delete every emoji. Convert "click here" links to descriptive anchors. Convert Title Case headings to sentence case. Render every quantity as a numeral.
- **Callouts.** Map asides onto `Tip:` for instructional content; keep the trust-statement box if the source makes any claim about how it was researched.

**How strictly to imitate:**

Imitate the synthetic samples at the level of sentence shape and paragraph length, not phrasing. Reuse the fixed strings (CTA labels, "Trusted by…", the closing sentences) only when the document genuinely has that slot; do not bolt a stat band, a trust strip, or a Challenge/Solution/Results triad onto a document with no content for it.

Three habits are non-negotiable, because without them the output reads as ordinary SaaS copy wearing this voice as a costume:

1. **Name the drudgery before naming the fix**, in concrete nouns the reader recognizes from their own week.
2. **Every claim ends on the reader's payoff**, usually through a `so you can` clause — not on the feature.
3. **Volunteer the limitation.** If the source names a drawback, a gap, or an "except when", keep it and place it in plain sight. This voice buys trust by conceding, and the templated pages it excludes are exactly the ones that never do.
