# Style: hubspot.com
Generated from 288 pages (100% of non-serial site coverage, 18 serial sections sampled; 237 corpus pages analyzed) on 2026-08-09.

## Voice profile
HubSpot writes as a large, plainspoken software company talking to one busy operator of a growing business — a marketer, rep, founder, or partner who owns a budget and a team. The reader is "you" and owns everything in the sentence ("your data", "your reps", "your pipeline"); the brand is usually **"HubSpot"** in the third person rather than "we", so the product name does the work of the subject. "We" is rationed and reserved for moments when the company takes on an obligation — migration, support, onboarding, a promise. The register is confident without boasting: every superlative is either a number with a footnote or a quote from a named customer, never an adjective HubSpot applies to itself. Warmth comes from contractions, from admitting the problem before selling the fix ("Growing a business is hard"), and from the occasional dry aside — never from enthusiasm.

## Tone rules
Do:
- Address the reader in second person and possessive: "your team", "your CRM", "your bottom line". Feature copy is verb-first imperative with the subject elided ("Track deals", "Qualify leads, book meetings, and scale one-to-one conversations").
- Name the brand in the third person for capability claims: "HubSpot's Smart CRM unifies your customer information", not "we unify".
- Open by naming the pain, plainly, before the product arrives. The canonical move is two blunt sentences: an admission that the reader's job is hard, then a promise that the software won't be one more thing making it harder.
- Turn every argument on an antithesis: "X, not Y" / "not just X, but Y" / "Stop X, start Y". This is the single most characteristic sentence shape on the site.
- Quantify instead of praising: "94% more deals closed after 6 months", "299,000+ customers in over 135 countries", "in weeks, not months". A claim without a figure reads off-brand.
- Attribute every stat: a parenthetical source, an asterisked footnote naming sample size and month, or a named customer with full job title and company.
- Put superlatives, enthusiasm, and any harsh word about a competitor inside a customer quote. "Clunky", "a Frankenstein monster", "game-changer", "gold standard" are all quoted, never authored.
- Concede a rival's real strength before out-scoping it: one sentence granting the competitor its niche, a short rhetorical pivot ("But …?"), then the claim that the bigger job is what HubSpot is built for.
- Write FAQ questions in the buyer's own first person ("Can I customize my Sales Hub account?", "How many users do I get with my plan?") and answer in you/we, opening with a bare verdict: "Yes." / "No." / "Absolutely."
- State limits, exclusions, and prices flatly, in the same neutral register as the benefits: a service page says outright that the offering is standardized, not tailored, and fine print names a required one-time fee with its exact dollar figure, uncushioned.
- Reuse fixed blocks verbatim — CTA strings, the proof band, the closing panel, boilerplate paragraphs. Rewriting them is off-style.

Don't:
- Never use hype adjectives: revolutionary, cutting-edge, game-changing, best-in-class, world-class, industry-leading, next-generation, state-of-the-art, bleeding-edge, turnkey, hassle-free.
- Never use consultant filler: synergy, disrupt, paradigm, delve, "in today's fast-paced world", "it's no secret that", "harness the power of".
- Never hedge in brand voice: no "we believe", "arguably", "perhaps", "it could be argued". Calibrated hedges ("typically", "may", "often") carry all real uncertainty.
- Never say "click here", never print a bare URL as a link label.
- Never name a competitor in order to insult it. Competitors are usually categories — "traditional CRMs", "legacy CRM", "point solutions", "most tools", "generic AI tools".
- Never use urgency or fear-selling. The strongest offer language allowed is a flat eligibility note — new customers only, select offers for a limited time — with no countdown and no scarcity theater.
- Never use "I" in brand voice — it exists only in FAQ question headings (the reader) and in quotes.
- Never use semicolons. The site writes a second sentence instead (0–1 semicolons per 20,000 words).
- Never use emoji.
- Never promise the future in fine print: pricing and legal copy uses "can" and "may", not "will".

## Lexicon
**The load-bearing phrase is "grow better"** — mission, verb, and sign-off at once: "help millions of organizations grow better", "let's start growing better together", "Grow better with HubSpot". Never "grow bigger", never "scale up" as a slogan. `scale` is the newer sibling verb and dominates AI-era and startup pages ("scale without adding headcount").

Preferred verbs: grow, scale, unify, connect, consolidate, centralize, automate, streamline, surface, prioritize, convert, nurture, close, resolve, track, free up, save time, show up (in answer engines), get up and running, act on.

Preferred nouns and framings: customer platform / **agentic customer platform**, context ("Growth Context", "the context advantage"), outcomes ("measured in outcomes, not output"), single source of truth, all in one place, one connected system, buying signals, go-to-market (GTM) teams, front office, book of business, ecosystem, marketplace, guidance (the services register's load-bearing noun).

Product nouns are Title Case proper nouns used unglossed, and their order is fixed: Marketing Hub, Sales Hub, Service Hub, Content Hub, Data Hub, Revenue Hub, Agent Hub, Smart CRM, Breeze / Breeze Assistant, HubSpot Credits, HubSpot AEO, Loop Marketing, Starter Customer Platform, HubSpot Academy, App Marketplace, INBOUND / UNBOUND, HubSpotter(s). The `®`/`™` marks appear only on the homepage and on a page's own Hub in the H1. **Individual agents are lowercase common nouns in body prose** ("customer agent", "prospecting agent", "data agent") and Title Case in card labels — the site is genuinely inconsistent here and reproduces the inconsistency.

The pain lexicon is a small closed set, reused as the setup for every pivot: scattered, disconnected, siloed, fragmented, patchwork, cobbled, clunky, spreadsheets, busywork, back-and-forth, guesswork, flying blind, falling through the cracks, "15+ disconnected tools".

Signature constructions:
- **Negative promise** — the benefit stated as an absence: "No credit card required." "No coding required." "No setup, no waiting." "No forms, no design skills, no prior marketing expertise required."
- **Time compression** — "in minutes, not months", "in weeks, not months", "in hours, not weeks", "in seconds", "up and running in days".
- **The less/more swap** — "less time building lists and more time closing deals".
- **The purpose hinge** — "so you can…", "so reps can…", "so your team…" is the standard feature→benefit joint (30+ per large page).
- **"actually" is the house intensifier**, the word that signals a claim isn't marketing: "what's actually happening", "outreach that actually converts", "content AI engines actually cite".

Words this author never uses: revolutionary, cutting-edge, game-changing, best-in-class, world-class, industry-leading, next-generation, state-of-the-art, synergy, disrupt, paradigm, delve, turnkey, hassle-free, ninja/rockstar/guru, "click here", "in today's fast-paced world". The jargon filter is tight but **not absolute, and it varies by page vintage**: `empower`, `delight`, `leverage`, `utilize`, `seamless`, `supercharge` and `unlock` survive on older free-tool pages, service/use-case pages and the `/startups/*` property, and are entirely scrubbed from the 2026 AI, pricing, and partner pages. When in doubt, write the newer register: no jargon at all.

## Rhythm & syntax
- Sentences run 10–25 words; corpus median is 14–16, and it drops with page recency — the newest AI, Spotlight and pricing pages run a median of 5–12 words. Paragraphs are 1–4 sentences, 20–70 words, and never longer.
- **The dominant paragraph shape is three beats, one sentence each: pain → mechanism → payoff.** Sentence 1 names the hours or deals the reader is losing, sentence 2 states what that time should be doing instead, sentence 3 introduces the feature that closes the gap automatically.
- **Punch then expand**: a three-to-six-word declarative — often a "Stop X." command — then a longer sentence that cashes it out, usually with an em-dash payoff naming what the reader gets or does next.
- **Antithesis carries the argument**: within one sentence ("built for sellers, not the IT team"), or as two mirrored micro-sentences where the legacy category gets the weak verb and the product gets the strong one — information vs intelligence, "bolt on" vs "built in", promises vs delivers.
- **Rule of three is near-compulsory**, usually verbs or gerunds: "market, sell, and serve", "attract, convert, and engage", "build awareness, grow revenue, and scale support", "unify your data, teams, and tools".
- **Fragment triplets do the headline work**: two-to-three-word fragments, each with its own period — anaphora on a repeated opener ("More …" three times), a verb-verb-destination sequence, or a trust ladder that starts small and ends at scale.
- **The em dash is the signature mark** and always carries the twist, the payoff, or the qualifier — typically a trailing "so …" clause naming who benefits, or a "not just X" narrowing. Spacing is inconsistent site-wide (spaced and unspaced both appear, sometimes on one page); pick one per document. En dashes are for ranges only.
- **Semicolons are effectively banned.** Colons open lists, glossary lines and label-value pairs ("The result:", "Best for:", "Price:").
- **Sentence-initial conjunctions are standard**: And, But, So, Plus, Then, Now, Today.
- **Rhetorical question as pivot**, answered immediately: "The result?" / "Out of the office? No problem." / "Need to go deeper?" / "What if you could…?" Questions cluster in headings and FAQs; they are rare mid-paragraph.
- Exclamation marks are rationed to near-zero in brand voice (roughly 8 per 24,000 words) and are common inside customer quotes and on community/careers pages.
- Numbers are always digits, never spelled out, except when opening a sentence. Multipliers `3x`, `5X`; counts with a `+` suffix ("299,000+", "2,000+"); percentages bare.

## Structure
- **The universal page skeleton**: eyebrow/kicker → H1 (often printed twice) → one-sentence value prop, 15–25 words → two CTAs, each with a lowercase subline on its own line → reassurance microcopy ("No credit card required. Free and premium features available.") → exactly three benefit bullets → the social-proof band (a single fixed sentence pairing the 299,000+ customer count with the 135-country count) → alternating feature blocks → stats trio → case study with "The Challenge" / "The Solution" labels → pricing ladder (Free / Starter / Professional / Enterprise) → "Related Resources" trio → FAQ → **closing CTA band that reprints the hero CTA verbatim, sublines and fine print included**.
- **Feature-section headings are complete sentences in sentence case ending with a period**: "Let payments collect themselves." "Follow up when you're top of mind." "Automate busy work with chatbots." Navigational and section headings are Title Case noun phrases with no period. The two systems never mix within a page.
- **ALL-CAPS eyebrow kickers** label blocks on newer pages: `USE CASES`, `BENEFITS`, `FEATURES`, `PRICING`, `CUSTOMER PROOF`, `WHY AEO MATTERS`, `Ecosystem ROI`.
- **CTA strings are a closed vocabulary, reused verbatim, never invented**: "Get started free", "Get a demo", "Start 14-day free trial", "Buy now", "Talk to Sales", "Learn more", "Request more information", "Read the full case study", "Calculate your price". Each pairs with a lowercase fragment subline naming the object ("Get a demo / of Revenue Hub"). The CTA ladder is a friction gradient: free tier → "Get started free"; paid → "Buy now"; enterprise → "Talk to Sales" only.
- **Openers never begin with the company.** They begin with an industry tension, a reader memory, or a broken status quo, then pivot with *Today* / *Now* / *So* / *With HubSpot,* to the product.
- **The FAQ is load-bearing, not an afterthought** — present on most pages, often longer than the body. Questions are literal search queries in the buyer's first person; answers open with a bare verdict and end in a route (a link, a tier, a calculator).
- **Block reuse is house style.** Whole modules — the closing CTA, the credits paragraph, the "Popular Features" mega-grid, the onboarding promise, the case-study CTA — appear byte-identical across dozens of pages. Reuse the fixed string; do not paraphrase it.
- Closings are always an imperative or aspirational fragment plus a link. No page ends on a summary or a sign-off.

## Formatting habits
- **No emoji.** The only exceptions in 237 pages are `💰` used as a discount-tier signal on the startups page and Slack emoji codes quoted inside an employee Q&A. Treat zero as the rule.
- **Bold is structural, not emphatic** — the label half of `Label: explanation` bullets, step names, and inline callouts (`Pro Tip:`, `Best for:`, `Note:`, `Please note:`). Italics are essentially unused. Emphasis is carried by heading level, fragment length, and the oversized stat figure.
- **Two bullet grammars, held consistently**: full-sentence bullets take a terminal period; bare noun-phrase and feature fragments take none. Label-first bullets are the default shape: a bolded feature label, a colon, then one full sentence telling "your team" what it can now do.
- **Numbered lists are reserved for procedures** and are usually rendered as `Step N: <Imperative>` headings rather than list markup. Frameworks are always exactly three steps.
- **Links are descriptive verb phrases**: "Read the full case study", "Explore our developer resources.", "Browse free courses", "Download the ROI report". Some link labels end with a period inside the label; an arrow (`→`) occasionally trails an in-FAQ link.
- **Stats are a two-line unit**: oversized bare figure, then a lowercase explanatory fragment ("97%" / "increase in deal close rate"), with an asterisked source or a "Download ROI report" link beneath.
- **Footnotes** hang off the claim with escalating markers (`*`, `**`, `***`, `^`) and name the sample and window in one flat sentence: which plan tiers, with or without the feature, and the month range measured.
- **Prices**: "$50/month", "Starts at $95/month", "$10/mo/seat", "(3 seats included)", discount and list price printed adjacent with no label, "Price shown in USD and subject to applicable tax." as the standard legal tail.
- **Beta status is inline and unhidden** — the site uses `(Beta)`, `BETA`, `[Beta]` and "Now in public beta." interchangeably. Pick one per document.
- **Trademark marks** (`®`, `™`) appear only on the homepage and on a page's own Hub name; drop them elsewhere.
- Dates are long-form in prose ("June 25, 2026"), slashed in listings ("4/14/26"); times spell the meridiem with periods and give both zones ("11:30 a.m.–12:30 p.m. ET / 8:30 a.m. PT").
- Ampersands appear in labels and nav; running prose always spells "and".

## Genre notes
Sub-profiles — only what differs from the base voice:

- **Product / free-tool pages**: the base register. Sentence-case feature headings with terminal periods, "Click to enlarge" captions, a phone-number nudge (a "Have questions?" opener plus an offer to walk you through it), a "Looking for enterprise features?" upsell, and a recycled feature mega-grid. Older tool pages still carry the retired adjectival voice ("in a flash", "beautiful", "watch as your conversion rates soar") — avoid it unless deliberately reproducing 2022-era copy.
- **2026 AI / Agent pages**: the tightest register on the site. No adjectives, median 5–14 word sentences, possessive hero headlines ("Your 24/7 AI Concierge"), and a reassurance ladder unique to this genre: "Nothing runs without your approval.", "Preview results before anything executes.", a start-small/build-trust/scale-later fragment triplet, and "at a pace that works for your team". Every agent page carries an "AI you can trust" block.
- **Newsroom / company news**: tension-first ledes that withhold the announcement to paragraph 2–4, then pivot with "Today, HubSpot is…". All bravado lives in an attributed exec quote (`"…," said <Full Name>, <Full Title> at <Company>.`). Fixed furniture: byline, "Updated:" date, audio duration, footnote run, `Topics:` tag, "Don't forget to share this post!"
- **Competitor comparison pages**: rigid template — `HubSpot vs <X>` → one-line positioning subhead → CTA pair → three stat tiles → switcher quotes → the same three benefit labels repeated as body headings, each conceding the rival then out-scoping it → FAQ in the buyer's voice → CTA → a dated accuracy disclaimer with numbered survey citations.
- **Case studies**: third person about the customer, past tense, zero editorial "we" — HubSpot is a supporting character in someone else's story. Open on the end-user's pain (the IT manager, the practice manager), never on the company. Labeled acts: The Background / The Challenge / The Solution / The Transformation / Looking Ahead. Subheads are story beats, not labels — a dramatic turning point phrased as a moment, not a section name. Every page closes with the same verbatim CTA block.
- **Careers, culture, and the careers blog**: the one place first-person plural is the default and the only place humor is allowed to breathe. Fixed vocabulary — HubSpotter, Culture Code, Culture Commitments, "Solve for the customer", @home/@office/@flex. Essays open by naming a common assumption and breaking it ("That framing is wrong."), admit the unglamorous ("We won't pretend this work is glamorous."), and close on mission plus a recruiting CTA.
- **Services / onboarding / consulting**: flatter and more literal than anything else on the site. "Guidance" is the load-bearing noun; the deliverable is "a dedicated <Role>". The defining trait is unapologetic expectation-setting — limits stated in the same neutral register as benefits, and even the CTA is conditional ("Talk to Sales to see if Premium Support can help you"). Fixed "Key Details" block: `Price:` → `Setting:` → `Timeline:`.
- **Spotlight (release microsite)**: fragment headlines that still take a terminal period ("Data work, done for you."), a per-edition keyword hammered throughout (context / all your data / speed), the five-slot update card (badge → Hub tags → name → one sentence → "Now in public beta."), and puns in headlines only ("Ctrl+Alt+Delight.", "Quotes at the speed of yes.").
- **Pricing**: a separate register — roughly 90% specification, ~400 words of prose per page. First person is essentially banned; the buyer's "I" outnumbers HubSpot's "we" ten to one, all in FAQ headings. Persuasion is delegated to badges (`Best Value`, `RECOMMENDED`, `BETA`) rather than adjectives. The tier-adjective ladder is fixed: Foundational → Essential → **Comprehensive** → **Our most powerful**. Inheritance is always "`<Product> <lower tier>, plus:`". Limits use `Up to <N>`, rates use `<N> per <unit>`, and "This feature uses HubSpot Credits." is a fixed sentence. Fine print sits under the thing it qualifies, marked by a lone asterisk, and states the negative plainly.
- **Partner / ecosystem pages**: reciprocity is the argument — a balanced "the more you …, the more we …" conditional trading the partner's customer value for HubSpot's investment. Program jargon runs unglossed (MDF, co-sell, GTM, tiers). Policy pages deliver rules with one deadpan aside — a dry complaint about people miscapitalizing the brand name, closing on a one-word sigh. Legal pages invert the whole voice: no contractions, "Partner" as a third-person proper noun, ALL-CAPS liability blocks, and a "Related Resources" bridge back to the marketing pages.
- **Resource library and template pages**: pure chrome with a swapped card deck. Card blurbs are the only writing that moves: a rhetorical question naming the reader's pain, then one sentence where the free template is the answer. Newer template pages add a **candid-limits move** — HubSpot enumerating its own free tool's ceilings in a three-item list, then converting the admission into the upsell.
- **`/startups/*`**: an editorial "we" the rest of the site doesn't use, a founder sublexicon (runway, headcount, pre-seed, ARR, PLG), the looser jargon filter, and the site's only emoji.

>
> Send reminders automatically. Log every reply. Get paid in days, not weeks.

> No spreadsheets required.

>

## Rewrite instructions
When restyling a foreign document into hubspot.com voice:

**Preserve from the source:** every fact, figure, price, date, name, product name, and technical claim, and the document's language (do not translate). Keep the structure of meaning — what is claimed, what supports it, what follows — and keep any legally required statement verbatim. Keep exact numbers exact: HubSpot's persuasion runs on figures, so precision in the source is an asset. If the source has no numbers, do not invent them; restructure around what it does assert.

**Replace:** the voice, rhythm, lexicon, and formatting. Concretely:
- Recast into second person with the reader as the operator ("your team", "your data"). Use the product or company name as the grammatical subject for capability claims instead of "we"; reserve "we" for promises, support, and onboarding.
- Break paragraphs to 1–3 sentences. Reshape each into pain → mechanism → payoff, or punch-then-expand.
- Convert every contrast into antithesis ("X, not Y"), every list of three into a verb triad, and every feature into a "so you can…" benefit hinge.
- Rewrite section headings as complete sentences in sentence case ending with a period; keep navigational headings as Title Case noun phrases without one.
- Replace hype adjectives with numbers and footnotes. Move any surviving superlative into an attributed quote, or delete it.
- Strip semicolons, emoji, "click here", hedges, and urgency. Keep contractions.
- Rewrite Q&A so the question is in the reader's first person and the answer opens with a bare "Yes." / "No." / "Absolutely."
- State limits, exclusions and prices in the same flat register as the benefits — do not soften or bury them.
- Apply the formatting habits: digits over words, em dash for the payoff clause, label-first bullets, descriptive link text, `Step N:` for procedures, the two-line stat unit.
- If the piece sells anything, use the fixed CTA strings verbatim ("Get started free", "Get a demo", "Talk to Sales", "Buy now") with a lowercase object subline, and reprint the opening CTA at the close rather than inventing a new sign-off.

**Imitate the synthetic samples strictly for register, rhythm, and structure — never copy their sentences.** They are cadence templates: match their sentence lengths, fragment triplets, em-dash payoffs, and verdict-first answers using the source document's own content.

**Pick the sub-profile by target document type:** landing or product page → Product/AI pages; competitor page → Comparison; customer story → Case study (third person, no "we", labeled acts); release note or changelog → Spotlight (fragment headlines with periods, badge + one sentence); press release or announcement → Newsroom (tension lede, exec quote carries the claim); job posting, values, or internal essay → Careers (first-person plural, break an assumption, admit the unglamorous); setup or professional-services description → Services (flat, limit-stating, "Key Details" block); price list or plan matrix → Pricing (no narrator, `Up to <N>` bounds, tier-adjective ladder, asterisked fine print); partner or program page → Partner (reciprocity framing, unglossed program jargon). If nothing fits, default to the base voice with the page skeleton removed: short paragraphs, second person, antithesis, numbers instead of adjectives, and no CTA.
