# Surfaces — craft rules per line

Load the section for the surface being written. The reader state and the budget for each one live in the SKILL.md table; this file is what to do with them. Everything here assumes the no-invention rule already holds: a number, a benefit or a name in any example below comes from the user.

## Headlines and page titles

The reader is mid-scroll and owes you nothing. Earn the stop with a concrete detail, not with energy.

- Lead with the sharpest concrete thing you have: a number, a name, an outcome, a contradiction. "We cut our AWS bill by $40,000 in one afternoon" stops someone; "How we optimized our cloud spend" does not.
- Use the reader's words, not the industry's. "Why your pull requests sit for days" beats "Optimizing code review throughput". The 11pm search query from the intake is the vocabulary source.
- A curiosity gap is allowed only where the piece closes it, and you withhold the answer rather than the subject. Naming the subject is what separates a promise from bait.
- Odd and verifiable beats round and inflated. "17 minutes" reads as measured; "in record time" reads as written by someone who did not measure.
- The title is read where nothing surrounds it — a search result, a tab, a file listing, a link someone pasted. It states its subject and says what happened to that subject. Three shapes that look like titles and are not: the unanchored fragment (a measurement with its subject cut, "Five seconds in under three"), the topic label (an area rather than a claim, "Notes on caching"), and the teaser written to withhold.
- One sentence's worth of meaning, and one sentence only. A headline with its own consequence welded on after a full stop is a headline plus the article's first line; the second half belongs in the opening paragraph.

## Short descriptions

Meta descriptions, store blurbs, product one-liners, social previews. One glance, then a decision.

- The first five words carry the benefit. Do not spend them on the product's name: it is already on the screen, above the text you are writing.
- Concrete nouns and verbs. "Turns receipts into a tax report" beats "streamlines your financial workflow", because the first one can be pictured and the second one cannot.
- One idea. Two benefits compete and the reader retains neither.
- Respect the budget by dropping ideas. A description compressed into fragments to fit reads like a machine that ran out of room.

## Microcopy

Buttons, empty states, errors, tooltips, labels, confirmations. Here the words *are* the interface and every one pays rent.

- Buttons name the result of the action: "Save draft", "Send invoice", "Delete account". Not "Submit", "OK", "Continue" where the action has a name.
- Errors say what went wrong, then how to fix it, and never blame the reader. "That card was declined. Try another card or check the number." Not "An error occurred", not "Invalid input", and not an apology in place of a fix. The reader's task is broken and they may already be blaming themselves; the copy's job is to take that back.
- Empty states sell the first action instead of describing the absence. "Add your first client to start invoicing" beats "No data to display". The reader is new and looking for the next step, not for confirmation that the screen is empty.
- Destructive confirmations state the consequence and its scope, with the number: "Delete 3 files? This cannot be undone."
- Tooltips explain what is not visible. A tooltip restating its own label is noise with a hover delay attached.
- Match the product's existing case and punctuation convention. Where there is none, sentence case, and no terminal period on labels or buttons.

## Subject lines and preview text

- Write to one person, not a segment. A subject line that reads like a colleague's email is opened; one that reads like a campaign is archived.
- Front-load the concrete word. The mobile preview cuts around 30 to 40 characters, so the payoff cannot sit at the end.
- Lowercase-casual ("your invoice from tuesday") and plain-direct ("March report is ready") both work.
- Fake urgency and fake familiarity buy one open and cost the next ten. "LAST CHANCE", "quick question", a fabricated re: — each trains the reader to skip you.
- Preview text extends the subject; it never repeats it. A preview echoing the subject wastes the second line the inbox gave you for free.

## CTAs

- Name what happens next, in the reader's terms: "Start the trial", "See the pricing", "Book a 20-minute call". Duration, cost and commitment in the label remove the hesitation that a vaguer label leaves in place.
- One primary action per view. Where the page genuinely needs several, one is primary and the rest are text links. (Whether the rendered page actually does this is `awesome-landing-audit`'s check, not this skill's.)
- First person possessive ("Start my trial") is a house-style choice, not an improvement. Follow the product's existing convention rather than switching it inside one line.

## Copy that recruits the next reader

Converting the reader in front of you is half of it; the other half is whether they carry the line to someone else. This is craft, not a separate deliverable — it decides which of two otherwise equal variants ships.

- Write lines a person can repeat. The test: could the reader quote this to a coworker an hour later, from memory? Repeatable beats clever.
- Give the reader cover to pass it on: a surprising number, a claim they would look informed forwarding, the thing everyone in the category thinks and nobody has written down.
- Every surface is an acquisition surface. Receipts, confirmation emails, error messages and empty states are read at full attention. One plain human line there does more than a banner.
- Where the product allows it, write the loop into the copy itself: "Invite your client so they can pay this invoice" turns one user's task into the next user's first contact.
- Never manufacture it. A share-me moment that is not true reads as promotional filler, and the detail that earns the share has to come from the user like every other fact.
